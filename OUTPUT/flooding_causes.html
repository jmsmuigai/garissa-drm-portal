Why Garissa Floods: The Anatomy of a Disaster

To the uninitiated, it seems like a paradox: How does one of Kenya's driest counties suffer from some of the nation's most catastrophic flooding? The answer lies in a complex interplay of upstream geography, human engineering, and local topography. When disaster strikes, it is rarely due to a single failure, but rather a cascade of events.

⚠️ Threat Vector 1: The River Tana & Upstream Rainfall

Garissa sits on the banks of the River Tana, Kenya's longest river. The Tana's catchment area begins hundreds of kilometers away in the central highlands (Mt. Kenya and the Aberdare Range). When heavy rains fall in central Kenya—often driven by El Niño—the runoff funnels into the Tana. Even if Garissa itself hasn't seen a drop of rain, the river can swell and burst its banks violently, inundating Garissa Town, Bura, and Masalani.

⚠️ Threat Vector 2: The Masinga Dam Spillway Cascade

To manage the Tana's flow and generate hydroelectric power, a cascade of dams (the Seven Forks Dams) was built upstream. Masinga Dam is the largest and acts as the primary reservoir. However, during extreme rain events, the reservoir reaches capacity. To prevent the dam from failing catastrophically, operators must open the spillways. This released water travels downstream, compounding with local runoff, and typically reaches Garissa within 4 to 5 days like a slow-moving tsunami.

⚠️ Threat Vector 3: Laghas (Seasonal Rivers)

Away from the Tana, Garissa is scarred by a network of dry riverbeds known as Laghas. For 90% of the year, these are sandy paths used for transport. But during sudden, intense local downpours, the baked, impermeable clay soils cannot absorb the water. Flash floods race down these laghas with terrifying speed, sweeping away livestock, vehicles, and temporary settlements. These flash floods isolate sub-counties like Lagdera and Fafi by washing away roads.

The Topographical Challenge

Garissa County is predominantly a flat, low-lying plain. Once the River Tana bursts its banks, there is no natural topography to contain the water. It spreads outward for kilometers, turning vast tracts of grazing land into shallow, stagnant lakes. This standing water becomes a breeding ground for mosquitoes, rapidly escalating the risk of waterborne diseases and Rift Valley Fever (RVF) for both humans and livestock.

Understanding these mechanisms is why GEWAS exists: by monitoring the Masinga Dam levels and upstream rainfall, we can issue warnings days before the floodwaters arrive at our doorstep.
